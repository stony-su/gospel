Let science be my gospel
and life be my creed.

Gospel builds a meal plan that repeats: the same dishes every week, fortnight or month, sized to your body from published dietary reference intakes and shopped for by a list that knows what is already in your cupboard. It runs on the phone alone. No account, no server, nothing leaves the device.

Solved once, then repeated.

Twelve questions. Six of them, sex, age, weight, diet, activity and sun, resolve forty-eight targets from published dietary reference intakes. The other six shape the cooking: cuisines, difficulty, minutes per meal, weekly budget, and how often the plan repeats.

The plan is solved on the phone against those targets, and then it stays put. Same answers, same plan. Regenerating is a deliberate act.

  • Plan Every meal of the cycle, with the portion that lands the day on its energy target.
  • Nutrition Forty-three targets against what the plan delivers, each summed from USDA FoodData Central over the ingredient weights.
  • Grocery One list for the cycle, by aisle, with the estimate.
  • Pantry What you already own, and when it runs out.

Any meal can be swapped.

A solver optimising forty-eight targets will sooner or later put something on a Tuesday you will not cook, and one such dish is enough to stop you following the plan at all. Swap opens every recipe that could take the slot, ranked by the planner's own objective, so the top of the list is what the solver would have chosen if it had looked harder.

Each card opens onto a radar of the dish against its share of the day. Choosing one rescales that day's portions so it still lands on its energy target. The other six days are left alone.

Every target shows its working: the rule that set it, how settled the standard is, and the papers it rests on. Twenty sources, cited in full.

A list that knows your cupboard.

The grocery list is priced, sorted by aisle, and ticks off what the pantry already holds. A 300 g tub of sour cream against 60 g a week is covered on quantity for five weeks, but its 21-day shelf life expires first, so it returns to the list in cycle four, and the pantry says why.

Prices, shelf lives and pack sizes are estimates by ingredient category. They are good enough to rank options and pace a shopping list, not to quote a bill, and the app says so.

Five hundred dishes, from public pages.

Nothing is bought or downloaded as a dataset. Each dish is assembled from three public Wikimedia pages, and the photographs are bundled rather than fetched, so a recipe cannot lose its picture to someone else's outage. Japanese, Italian, American, French and Spanish carry the library, with everyday cooking from seventeen more cuisines.

No recipe publishes its own nutrition, so every figure on a card is computed from FoodData Central panels over the ingredient weights. Each recipe carries the share of its mass that matched a panel, and shows it.

The interface has no colour.

The palette is one neutral ramp from black to white, and everything the app needs to say, a target met, a limit breached, a selection, a threshold, is said with fill, weight, hatch or inversion instead. Form survives a greyscale screenshot, a colour-blind reader and direct sunlight. Depth does the work colour would: four surface levels, near-black, and a level means the same thing everywhere.

The photographs are the one exception, and they are in full colour. A plate of food is the only thing on screen that is not a measurement, and whether a curry is deep red or pale yellow is the point of the picture.

Install it.

Gospel is an Android app and is not on Google Play. Download the file on your phone, open it, and allow the install when Android asks. It runs offline from the first screen, so there is nothing to sign in to.
